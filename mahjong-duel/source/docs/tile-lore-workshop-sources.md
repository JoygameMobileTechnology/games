# Dutch Golden Age and Brass Meridian tile notes

The one-sentence notes in `src/tile-lore-workshop.js` combine historical context with readings written for these game illustrations. They do not claim that each object has a universal, fixed symbolic meaning. Notes introduced with “Here”, “this imagined”, or a reference to the game’s theme are interpretations, not reports of established historical iconography. Number and tier variants distinguish the artwork without assigning invented numerological meanings.

Reviewed 25 September 2026. All source prose was paraphrased; no museum text is quoted in the game.

## Dutch Golden Age

- [National Gallery, Harmen Steenwyck, *Still Life: An Allegory of the Vanities of Human Life*](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/harmen-steenwyck-still-life-an-allegory-of-the-vanities-of-human-life): supports the general vanitas setting, timepieces, learning, temporary musical pleasures, faith and worldly possessions; it also cautions that the painter’s specific reasons can be uncertain. Used for Pocket watch, Violin, Open folio, Music score scroll, Cross and the broader interpretive frame of Gold urn, Jewel chest, Coins, Mirror, Hourglass and Pedestal tiers.
- [Metropolitan Museum, Jacques de Gheyn II, *Vanitas Still Life*](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/436485): independent example of objects associated with life’s brevity; supports the general context rather than assigning a meaning to every object in this game.
- [National Gallery of Art, *Banquet Piece with Mince Pie*](https://www.nga.gov/research/publications/online-editions/dutch-paintings-seventeenth-century-banquet-piece-mince-pie): supports the constructed nature of banquet still lifes, lemon peel as a spatial device, candles as a time reminder and the possible Christian dimension of food. Used for Peeled lemon, Candelabrum, Bread loaf and Goblet context.
- [National Gallery of Art, *Still Life with Peacock Pie*](https://www.nga.gov/research/publications/online-editions/dutch-paintings-seventeenth-century-still-life-peacock-pie): describes a lavish still life as a celebration of prosperity without obvious transience symbolism. Supports Lobster and the decision not to force vanitas symbolism onto every edible object.
- [Rijksmuseum, Delftware](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/node/Delftware--9796684f8d234ce4a661750b1a3a1748): supports the relationship of Dutch blue-and-white tin-glazed earthenware to Chinese porcelain imports. Used for Delft ginger jar.
- [National Gallery of Art, *Nautilus Cup*](https://www.nga.gov/artworks/225617-nautilus-cup): explains collecting natural marvels, European metal mounts and Indo-Pacific shells carried on Dutch trade ships. Used for Nautilus cup and the Cabinet-of-curiosities interpretation of Coral branch; the coral reading is not claimed as a conventional coded symbol.
- [National Gallery of Art, *From Botany to Bouquets* catalogue](https://www.nga.gov/sites/default/files/migrate_images/content/dam/ngaweb/research/publications/pdfs/from-botany-to-bouquets.pdf): supports Dutch botanical collecting and interest in rare tulips during the 1630s. The game does not repeat sensational specific price claims. Used for Striped tulip.
- [V&A, *Paper and Pearls: Hans Collaert’s Designs for Pendants*](https://www.vam.ac.uk/blog/engraved-ornament-project/paper-and-pearls-hans-collaerts-designs-jewelry): supports seventeenth-century pearl rarity, portrait status and overseas sourcing/diving. Used for Pearl necklace and Pearl count variants; collecting and satisfaction language in those variants is an interpretive extension.
- [Rijksmuseum, *Portrait of Catharina Fourmenois*](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/object/Portrait%2Bof%2BCatharina%2BFourmenois%2B%281598-1665%29--ba4558289cad6a98f569b35d1ad26432): discusses jewellery, lace and expensive clothing in Dutch portraiture. Used for Brooch, Lace collar and the interpretive dress context of Cushion, Fan, Ribbon, Glove, Purse and Scissors.
- [National Gallery of Art, *Still Life with Fruit*, 1675](https://www.nga.gov/research/publications/online-editions/dutch-paintings-seventeenth-century-still-life-fruit-1675): supports grapes/wine as Eucharistic imagery and pomegranate seeds as the unity of the Church in a particular Christian still life. Game wording deliberately says “could” and “in some” rather than asserting every fruit image has that meaning.
- [Mauritshuis, Jan Steen, *Girl Eating Oysters*](https://www.mauritshuis.nl/en/our-collection/artworks/818-girl-eating-oysters): supports the oyster’s amorous associations in Dutch painting. Game copy uses a gentle courtship description.
- [Rijksmuseum, *Model van een watermolen WIP*](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/object/Model-van-een-watermolen-WIP--01b8b01536917e8d761fee06dec6c968): supports windmills pumping water from reclaimed land. Used for Windmill.

Additional interpretive notes: Fig, Feather, Perfume, Spoon, Key, Walnut, Seal, Bee, Pipe, Sun, Crescent, Heart, Fleur-de-lis, Star, Chess knight, Mortar and all Pedestal tiers offer readings within the game’s Dutch collection, not claims of a historical iconographic code. Globe, Quill and Ship situate the objects in trade, learning and correspondence. The fleur-de-lis sentence evokes heraldic prestige without inventing a specifically Dutch religious or dynastic meaning. The pipe sentence treats disappearing smoke as an analogy rather than claiming a meaning inherent in every Dutch pipe image.

## Brass Meridian

Brass Meridian is a fictional steampunk setting. Its automata and stacked boiler variants are not historical artefacts. The notes explicitly describe these as imagined, and the symbolic readings of ordinary tools belong to this fictional workshop.

- [Royal Museums Greenwich, *Binnacle and compass*](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-7116): explains compass deviation caused by nineteenth-century iron ships and attempts to correct it. Used for Compass.
- [Royal Museums Greenwich, *Christmas items in the collections* — sextant section](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/art-culture/christmas-items-royal-museums-greenwichs-collections): explains measuring celestial angles for navigation. Used for Sextant, with related interpretive Sun/Star notes.
- [Royal Museums Greenwich, *A history of the Royal Observatory in six objects*](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/space-astronomy/history-royal-observatory-six-objects): documents observations, telescopes, navigation and timekeeping. Used for Telescope’s historical context.
- [Royal Museums Greenwich, *Armillary sphere*](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-10790): describes rings representing astronomical relationships. Used for Armillary sphere.
- [Science Museum Group, *Rotative Steam Engine by Boulton and Watt, 1788*](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co50948/rotative-steam-engine-by-boulton-and-watt-1788): explains piston motion, rotative machinery, the centrifugal governor and steam’s industrial role. Used for Piston, Governor, Boiler and Locomotive context.
- [Science Museum Group, *Drawing of James Watt’s Pumping Engine 1788*](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co50960/drawing-of-james-watts-pumping-engine-1788-drawing): details valves admitting and controlling steam. Used for Valve.
- [Science Museum, *Steampunk in the Science Museum*](https://blog.sciencemuseum.org.uk/steampunk-in-the-science-museum/): distinguishes imaginative steampunk appeal from historical engineering and discusses governors controlling engine speed. Used for Governor context and the overall decision to label fictional readings.
- [Smithsonian National Museum of American History, *Telegraph Key*](https://americanhistory.si.edu/collections/object/nmah_890801): supports keys sending electrical pulses through wires. Used for Telegraph key; the game does not assign a particular Morse or submarine signalling mechanism to the unspecified illustration.
- [Science Museum Group, *Meteorological Office aneroid barometer Mark I*](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co429119/meteorological-office-aneroid-barometer-mark-i): supports atmospheric-pressure measurement. The game’s “Pendulum barometer” is explicitly an imagined hybrid, not represented as an authentic historical type.
- [Science Museum Group, *Mercurial vacuum gauge for beam engine*](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co51653/mercurial-vaccum-gauge-for-beam-engine): documents gauges used to read engine conditions and mentions the corresponding pressure gauge. Used only for the broad Pressure gauge context, not to imply that the tile is this particular instrument.

The remaining Brass Meridian notes are readings of function in the fictional theme: ambition, responsibility, repair, craft, learning from nature, recording and travel. The mechanical owl, crab, dragonfly and seahorse have no asserted traditional symbolic meanings. Airship, propeller, goggles, toolbox and boiler tiers are deliberately treated as imagined workshop motifs rather than attributed to a named inventor, date or lost tradition.
